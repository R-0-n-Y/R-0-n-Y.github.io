Research

Black hole accretion flows are the connective tissue of my work. Everything below is a different observational window onto the same object: hot, magnetized plasma falling through strong-field gravity.

Magnetized accretion flows in strong gravity

Most analytic accretion theory assumes either a thin Keplerian disk or a purely hydrodynamic advective flow. Real flows around black holes are neither: they are transonic, magnetized, and often carry low angular momentum. I have built global steady-state general relativistic magnetohydrodynamic (GRMHD) solutions in Kerr spacetime that admit standing shocks — discontinuities where the flow decelerates abruptly, heats up, and becomes a natural site for hard X-ray emission and jet launching.

This programme runs from the analytic side (critical-point analysis, shock conditions, parameter-space topology in spin and magnetization) through to full time-dependent simulation, testing whether the structures the semi-analytic theory predicts actually survive in a turbulent flow.

From flow structure to horizon-scale images

A model of an accretion flow is only useful if it can be confronted with data. I use general relativistic ray-tracing to convert flow solutions and GRMHD simulation output into the quantities the Event Horizon Telescope and its successors actually measure: image morphology, ring structure, and linear and circular polarization.

Two questions drive the current work. First, how do turbulent magnetic fields — as opposed to the smooth, ordered fields usually assumed — alter horizon-scale images? Second, what do trans-magnetosonic, low-angular-momentum flows look like when imaged, and can that distinguish them from the standard magnetically arrested picture? Work on the latter is being carried out with the Niels Bohr Institute.

Variability: QPOs, state transitions and flares

Black hole X-ray binaries change state on human timescales, and quasi-periodic oscillations track those changes. Shock-bounded post-shock regions provide a physically motivated oscillator; I work on connecting their dynamics to observed QPO frequencies and to the hard–soft state transitions of BH-XRBs.

The same physics applies at the Galactic Centre. Recent work argues that low-angular-momentum accretion shocks provide a sufficient energy reservoir to power the X-ray flares of Sgr A*, and extends this to the near- and mid-infrared flare population.

Gravitational waves and turbulent disks

Turbulence in an accretion disk is a stochastic, anisotropic mass-energy distribution close to a black hole — which means it both emits gravitational waves and affects those passing through it. I am working on both directions: the stochastic gravitational-wave background produced by turbulent disks around supermassive black holes, and the damping a gravitational wave experiences propagating through a viscous accreting medium. Related work looks at quasi-normal mode excitation driven directly by turbulence in GRMHD simulations.

The X-ray–radio fundamental plane

The fundamental plane of black hole activity relates X-ray luminosity, radio luminosity and black hole mass across nine orders of magnitude, but it remains largely an empirical scaling. I am developing a theoretical model grounded in shocked, magnetized accretion flow physics that aims to derive the plane rather than fit it — and, in doing so, to explain the scatter.

Cluster environments and dark matter

A more recent line of work uses galaxy clusters as particle-physics laboratories: radio synchrotron limits on WIMP annihilation in the Frontier Fields cluster Abell 2744, and broadband constraints on axion-like particle dark matter from the Coma cluster radio halo, with an eye toward what the SKA will be able to say. A parallel thread connects X-ray cavities in brightest cluster galaxies to finite-angular-momentum GRMHD inflows.
